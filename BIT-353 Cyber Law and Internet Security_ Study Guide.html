Teaching notes for Unit I: explained in simple words

1. What is e-commerce?

Think of your local market. You walk to a shop, look at goods, bargain, pay cash and carry the item home. Now think of ordering the same item on your phone: you look at pictures, pay with UPI and the item comes to your door. That second way is e-commerce (electronic commerce): doing business, meaning buying, selling, paying and servicing, through computers and networks, mostly the internet. It started in the 1970s with EDI and electronic fund transfer between banks, grew in the 1990s with the web (Amazon and eBay began in 1994 to 1995), and exploded in India after cheap 4G data and UPI. E-business is a wider word. It means running every activity of a firm electronically: buying raw material, managing stock, paying salaries, talking to customers, as well as selling. So e-commerce is only the selling-and-buying part of e-business.

2. Types of e-commerce

Types are decided by who sells to whom. In B2C (Business to Consumer) a company sells directly to an ordinary customer, such as Amazon, Flipkart, Myntra, BookMyShow. This is the type everyone knows. In B2B (Business to Business) one business sells to another, like a factory buying steel or a shopkeeper buying stock on IndiaMART or Udaan. Orders are big, prices are negotiated, and it is actually larger in money than B2C. In C2C (Consumer to Consumer) one person sells to another through a platform that acts as a middleman, as on OLX or Quikr for second-hand goods. In C2B (Consumer to Business) the individual offers something to a company, for example a freelancer selling design work on Upwork, or a photographer selling photos to a stock website. In B2G (Business to Government) companies sell to government offices, as in the GeM (Government e-Marketplace) portal and e-tendering. In G2C (Government to Citizen) the government gives services online: income tax filing, passport, railway reservation on IRCTC, DigiLocker. Newer forms are m-commerce (shopping through mobile apps), social commerce (selling on Instagram and WhatsApp), and D2C (a brand selling directly without middlemen).

3. Benefits, advantages and disadvantages

For the customer the advantages are convenience (shop from home at any time, even at midnight), a very wide choice, easy comparison of prices and reviews, discounts, and saving travel time. For the seller the shop is open 24x7, the market is the whole country or the world, there is no costly shop rent, stock and staff cost is lower, and the data of customers helps in making better offers. For the society it creates jobs in delivery, packaging and IT, and brings products to small towns. Disadvantages: the customer cannot touch, try or check the quality, and may receive a wrong or damaged item; there is the risk of fraud, fake websites, card theft and identity theft; privacy is at risk because companies collect data; delivery can be late and returns can be troublesome; people without internet or digital skill are left out; and small local shops lose business to big platforms. For the seller there is heavy competition, high advertising and logistics cost, and the need to follow tax and consumer laws.

4. Impact of e-commerce on business

E-commerce has changed the way a business works. The middlemen (wholesaler, distributor) are cut out, so goods become cheaper. New business models arrived: marketplaces, subscription services, food delivery, online education. A small producer from a village can reach customers in the whole country without opening a shop. Firms use customer data to recommend products and to fix prices. Competition increased, since customers can compare prices in seconds, so traditional shops must either go online or improve service. Business processes became faster because orders, invoices and payments are automatic, which reduces paperwork and mistakes. The risks also grew: cyber attacks, data leaks, dependence on the internet, and the need for new laws on taxes and consumer rights.

5. Network infrastructure: Internet, Intranet and Extranet

E-commerce stands on a network. The Internet is the public worldwide network of computer networks that all follow the TCP/IP rules. Anyone can join it by taking a connection from an ISP. It carries the web shops, e-mails and payments between customers and businesses. An Intranet is a private network inside one organisation that uses the same technologies as the internet (web pages, e-mail, browsers) but is closed to outsiders. A college portal that works only inside the campus network, or a company system for leave, salary and stock, is an intranet. It is protected by a firewall and logins, so it is safer and faster. An Extranet is an intranet that has been opened in a controlled way to selected outsiders such as suppliers, dealers or customers, who log in to see only the part meant for them, for example a car company letting its parts suppliers check orders. A simple way to teach: the Internet is a public road, an Intranet is the private compound of a factory, and an Extranet is the factory gate which opens only for approved visitors.

6. E-commerce: issues, problems and prospects

Issues are matters that need careful handling. Security: how to keep card numbers and passwords safe. Privacy: how the customer data is used. Trust and authentication: is the seller real, is the buyer genuine? Legal: whether an online contract is valid, what law applies to a seller in another country, and how GST and income tax are collected. Consumer protection: refund, return and fake reviews. Problems are practical difficulties: weak internet in villages, low digital literacy, fear of online fraud, preference for cash on delivery, costly and slow logistics in remote areas, language barriers, failed payments and the high return rate. Prospects are bright in India. UPI makes small payments free and instant. 4G and 5G data is cheap. Millions of first-time users are coming online from small towns. Government support through Digital India, ONDC (an open network where any small seller can be found through any buyer app), GeM and BharatNet is improving things. New fields such as online education, telemedicine, agri-trading and cross-border exports are growing.

7. Network access equipment

To reach the internet a user needs devices that connect and carry the signals. A modem (modulator-demodulator) converts the digital data of the computer into signals that a telephone line, cable or fibre can carry, and converts them back at the other end. A router is the traffic manager: it reads the destination address of each packet and sends it on the best path between networks, and a home Wi-Fi box is usually a modem and router together. A switch connects many computers within one LAN and sends data only to the device that needs it, while an old hub blindly sends it to everyone. A wireless access point lets laptops and phones join by radio (Wi-Fi). A network interface card (NIC) inside the computer gives it a physical address (MAC) and a port to join the network. A repeater boosts a weak signal, a bridge joins two LANs and a gateway joins networks that use different protocols. Cables used are twisted pair, coaxial and optical fibre; fibre is the fastest and the most reliable for long distance.

8. Broadband

In the early days, people used dial-up internet through a phone line and a modem. It was very slow (56 kbps), and while it was in use the phone was engaged. Broadband means a high-speed, always-on internet connection, where one line can carry data, voice and video at the same time. Technologies: DSL or ADSL over copper telephone wire; cable broadband over the TV cable; fibre to the home (FTTH), which uses light in glass strands and gives speeds from 100 Mbps to 1 Gbps; fixed wireless and mobile broadband (4G and 5G); and satellite (VSAT) for remote places, though the signal takes longer to travel. Broadband made online shopping, video calls, streaming, cloud storage, online classes and digital payments possible, which is why it is the basic requirement for e-commerce. India has a large broadband base, mainly through mobile networks, and the BharatNet project is laying fibre to village panchayats.

9. Telecommunication

Telecommunication means sending information (voice, text, pictures, video) over a distance using electrical, light or radio signals. It is the physical base on which the internet runs: telephone lines, undersea cables, optical fibre backbones, microwave towers, satellites and mobile towers. The main parts of any communication system are the sender, the message, the medium (channel), the receiver and the protocol (agreed rules). Mobile networks have moved through generations: 1G analogue voice; 2G digital voice and SMS (GSM); 3G mobile internet and video calls; 4G fast broadband that created the app economy; 5G very high speed, very low delay and support for billions of connected devices (IoT), launched in India in 2022. In India the sector is regulated by the Department of Telecommunications (DoT) and TRAI (Telecom Regulatory Authority of India), which sets rules on tariffs and quality of service. Good telecommunication means e-commerce works even in small towns.

Web security and firewalls

Security issues on web, importance of firewall, components of firewall, transaction security, emerging client-server, security threats, network security, factors in firewall design, limitations of firewalls.

Security issues on the web

The web was built to share, not to protect. Every request crosses networks you do not control, so three goals matter, called the CIA triad: Confidentiality (only the right people read it), Integrity (data is not changed), Availability (service is up when needed). Add authentication (who are you?) and non-repudiation (you cannot deny you did it).

  • Client side: malicious scripts, cookie theft, phishing, malware downloads.
  • Server side: SQL injection, XSS, weak passwords, misconfiguration, DoS and DDoS.
  • In transit: eavesdropping, man-in-the-middle, session hijacking. Fixed with HTTPS (TLS).

Firewall: importance and components

A firewall is a barrier between a trusted network (your LAN) and an untrusted one (the internet). It checks every packet against rules and allows or blocks it. Like a security guard at the gate with a list of who may enter.

Importance: blocks unauthorised access, hides internal addresses, logs traffic, enforces policy, single point to guard.

Transaction security and client-server threats

An online payment must be confidential (card number hidden), authentic (real merchant, real buyer), intact and non-deniable. Tools: SSL/TLS (HTTPS padlock), SET (older card standard), OTP and two-factor authentication, tokenisation, 3-D Secure, payment gateways, digital certificates.

Emerging client-server: in the old two-tier model the client talked directly to the database. Modern designs are three-tier or web-based (browser, application server, database), plus cloud and mobile apps. More tiers mean more places to attack, so each needs its own protection.

Network security means protecting data and devices on a network: firewalls, IDS/IPS (detect and prevent intrusions), antivirus, VPN, access control, patching, encryption, monitoring.

  • Services to allow (web, mail, VPN) and who needs remote access.

Detailed exam answers for Unit II (write these as full-length answers)

1. Security issues on the web

The World Wide Web was designed to make sharing of information easy, and security was not part of the original design. A web transaction passes through many computers and networks that the user and the merchant do not control, so information can be read, copied or altered on the way. Web security problems can be studied in three places: the client, the network and the server. On the client side, the browser can be attacked by malicious scripts, harmful downloads, fake websites (phishing) and stolen cookies, and the user may be tricked by social engineering. In the network, data travelling in plain text can be captured by sniffing, redirected by spoofing, or changed by a man-in-the-middle attacker who sits between the browser and the server. On the server side, the web server and its database can be attacked by SQL injection, cross-site scripting, defacement, weak passwords, unpatched software and denial-of-service floods. Every secure system tries to achieve confidentiality, integrity and availability, together with authentication and non-repudiation, and the later topics of this unit show the tools used for each.

2. Firewall: meaning, working and importance

A firewall is a hardware device, a software program, or a combination of both, placed at the boundary between a trusted internal network and an untrusted external network such as the Internet. It examines every packet that tries to pass, compares it with a set of rules called the security policy, and then permits, rejects or silently drops it. The word comes from the wall in a building that stops a fire from spreading from one section to another. In the same way, the firewall limits the damage from the outside world reaching the inside network. The rules usually look at the source address, destination address, port number, protocol and sometimes the content of the packet. A good firewall follows the principle of default deny: everything is blocked unless it is clearly allowed.

The importance of a firewall is that it gives one controlled entry point where security can be enforced and monitored. It prevents unauthorised users from reaching internal machines, hides the structure and addresses of the private network (using network address translation), filters harmful or unwanted traffic, records all connection attempts in logs for audit, supports VPN access for remote employees, and reduces the cost of protecting each machine separately. Without a firewall, every computer on the network would be directly exposed to scanning and attack.

3. Components of a firewall

A firewall installation is made from several cooperating components. The first is the packet-filtering router (screening router), which checks the header of each packet for IP addresses, ports and protocol and applies allow or deny rules at high speed, but it cannot understand the data inside the packet. The second is the application-level gateway or proxy server, which accepts the connection from the client, examines the request at the application level (for HTTP, FTP or mail), and opens a separate connection to the real server, so the outside party never talks directly to the internal machine. The third is the circuit-level gateway, which only checks that a TCP connection is set up properly and then relays the data. The fourth is the bastion host, a very strongly secured computer with unnecessary services removed, which sits on the edge and is expected to be attacked. The fifth is the demilitarised zone (DMZ), a separate small network between two firewalls where public servers such as the web server, mail server and DNS are kept, so that even if one of them is compromised, the inner network is not reached. Finally, logging and alerting tools record events and warn the administrator about suspicious behaviour.

4. Types and architectures of firewalls

Firewalls are classified by how deeply they inspect traffic. A stateless packet filter treats every packet on its own and is fast but easy to fool. A stateful inspection firewall remembers the state of each connection, so it allows an incoming reply only when an inside user has first made a request. A proxy firewall works at the application layer and gives the deepest checking at the cost of speed. A next-generation firewall combines these with deep packet inspection, intrusion prevention, application awareness and user identity. In terms of architecture, a dual-homed host has two network cards and no direct routing between them; a screened-host design places a packet filter in front of a bastion host; and a screened-subnet design uses two filtering routers with a DMZ between them, which is the most secure of the three.

5. Transaction security

When a customer pays online, the transaction must satisfy four requirements: confidentiality, so that card and account details are not seen by outsiders; integrity, so that the amount and the account number are not changed; authentication, so that the customer is sure the shop is genuine and the shop is sure the buyer is genuine; and non-repudiation, so that neither party can later deny the transaction. These are achieved mainly with the SSL/TLS protocol, which is shown by HTTPS and the padlock in the browser. During the TLS handshake the server sends its digital certificate, the browser verifies it with the trusted certifying authority, both sides agree on a session key using public key cryptography, and the rest of the conversation is encrypted with this fast symmetric key. Additional protection comes from one-time passwords and two-factor authentication required by the RBI, tokenisation of card numbers, the 3-D Secure verification page, secure payment gateways, and the PCI-DSS standard that merchants must follow. The older SET protocol was designed for card payments but was too complex and is now rarely used.

6. Emerging client-server model and its security threats

In the traditional two-tier client-server model, a client program on the desktop communicated directly with a database server. This was simple but difficult to secure and to update. The modern model is multi-tier: a thin client (a web browser or mobile app) talks to a web server, which talks to an application server, which talks to the database. Cloud computing, web services, APIs and mobile apps have extended the model further. Each tier adds flexibility, but it also adds a new point where an attacker may enter, so every layer needs authentication, authorisation, encryption and monitoring. Major threats include spoofing of identity, sniffing of data, session hijacking, SQL injection, cross-site scripting, buffer overflow, malware on the client, denial of service on the server and misuse of APIs. Defences are input validation, secure coding, regular patching, firewalls, intrusion detection, strong authentication and least privilege access.

7. Network security

Network security means the policies and technical measures used to prevent unauthorised access, misuse, modification or denial of a computer network and the data on it. It is built in layers, a strategy called defence in depth. The layers include physical security of equipment, access control with passwords and biometrics, firewalls at the boundary, intrusion detection and prevention systems that watch for attack patterns, antivirus and anti-malware software, encryption of data in storage and in transit, virtual private networks for remote access, network segmentation using VLANs, regular patching and backups, and user awareness training. A written security policy and periodic security audits make sure that the measures are used correctly.

8. Factors to be considered in firewall design

Before a firewall is installed, the organisation must first decide its security policy: which services are to be allowed, to whom, and in which direction. The design should follow default deny and give the minimum privilege needed. The network topology must be studied to decide where to place the firewall and the DMZ. The right type of firewall must be selected by comparing the level of protection required with its cost and speed, because a deep inspection firewall can become a bottleneck if it is too weak for the traffic. Other factors are the services needed by users (web, mail, remote login, VPN), the ease of management and rule updates, logging and monitoring ability, redundancy through a backup firewall for high availability, scalability for future growth, compatibility with existing equipment, legal and audit requirements, and the skill of the staff who will manage it.

9. Limitations of firewalls

A firewall is necessary but not sufficient. It cannot protect against attacks from inside the organisation, such as a dishonest employee or a careless user, because internal traffic does not cross it. It cannot stop traffic that bypasses it, for example through a modem, a pen drive or an unauthorised wireless access point. It cannot completely stop viruses, since scanning every file and every encrypted connection is difficult. It cannot defend against social engineering, weak passwords or software bugs in allowed services. A wrongly configured rule can leave a serious hole, while overly strict rules block legitimate work. It can slow down the network and creates a single point of failure if no backup exists. For these reasons a firewall must be combined with antivirus, intrusion detection, encryption, patching, user training and a strong security policy.

Encryption and digital signatures

Encryption techniques, symmetric keys and DES, triple DES, asymmetric encryption, secret key and public/private key encryption, digital signatures, VPN, technical and legal issues, electronic records, digital contracts, requirements of a digital signature system.

Encryption basics

Encryption scrambles readable plaintext into unreadable ciphertext using an algorithm and a key. Decryption reverses it. Only someone with the right key can read it.

  • Substitution cipher: replace letters. Caesar shift by 3: HELLO becomes KHOOR.
  • Transposition cipher: rearrange letters (columns, rails).
  • Modern ciphers combine both many times. Block ciphers work on fixed chunks, stream ciphers bit by bit.

Symmetric (secret key) encryption: DES and Triple DES

The same key encrypts and decrypts. Fast, good for large data. Problem: how do two strangers share the key safely (key distribution)? With n people you need n(n-1)/2 keys.

DES (Data Encryption Standard, from IBM, adopted 1977): initial permutation, 16 rounds each using a 48-bit subkey, then final permutation. Its 56-bit key is too short today. Triple DES runs DES three times with two or three keys to stretch the key length. Other symmetric ciphers: AES, Blowfish, RC4, IDEA.

DES and 3DES are replaced by AES (Rijndael, standard since 2001). Learn DES for the exam; use AES in practice.

Asymmetric (public and private key) encryption

Each user has a key pair: a public key (shared with everyone) and a private key (kept secret). What one locks, only the other opens. Like a mailbox: anyone can post a letter in (public key), only the owner has the key to open it (private key).

RSA (Rivest, Shamir, Adleman, 1977) rests on the difficulty of factoring a product of two huge primes. Toy example: p=3, q=11, n=33, phi=20, e=7, d=3. Encrypt M=4: C = 47 mod 33 = 16. Decrypt: 163 mod 33 = 4.

Digital signatures

A digital signature is the electronic equivalent of a handwritten signature: it proves who sent a message (authentication), that it was not altered (integrity) and that the sender cannot deny it (non-repudiation).

  • 1. HashMessage goes through SHA-256 to give a short digest
  • 2. SignDigest encrypted with the sender private key = signature
  • 3. SendMessage plus signature plus certificate
  • 4. VerifyReceiver decrypts the signature with the public key and compares to a fresh hash

If both digests match, the message is genuine and unchanged. A digital certificate (X.509), issued by a Certifying Authority (CA), binds a public key to a named person. In India CAs are licensed under the Controller of Certifying Authorities (CCA). Signing uses the private key; encrypting for secrecy uses the receiver public key. Do not mix them up.

Technical issues

  • Private key theft or loss, weak key length, hash collisions (MD5 and SHA-1 are broken), expired or revoked certificates, trust in the CA, timestamping, long-term validity.

Legal issues, electronic records and digital contracts

  • IT Act 2000, Sec 3: authentication of an electronic record by a digital signature (originally using asymmetric crypto and hash; later widened to electronic signatures, Sec 3A, such as Aadhaar eSign).
  • Sec 4 and 5: electronic records and e-signatures have legal recognition equal to paper and handwritten signatures.
  • Sec 10A: a contract is not invalid only because it was made electronically.
  • Evidence: electronic records are admissible with a certificate (earlier Sec 65B Indian Evidence Act, now Sec 63 Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam 2023).
  • Exceptions: wills, negotiable instruments other than cheques, powers of attorney, trusts and sale of immovable property still need paper (First Schedule).

Requirements of a digital signature system

  • Unique to the signer and under the signer sole control; verifiable by anyone.
  • Linked to the data so any change invalidates it; hard to forge.
  • Standard algorithms (RSA/ECDSA, SHA-2), a certificate authority and PKI, secure key storage (token, smart card), revocation lists, timestamping.

Virtual Private Network (VPN)

A VPN builds an encrypted tunnel across the public internet so remote users or branch offices behave as if on one private network. Protocols: IPsec, SSL/TLS VPN, L2TP, WireGuard (PPTP is outdated and weak). Benefits: confidentiality, remote work, cheaper than leased lines, hides your IP from sites. Limits: slower, trusts the VPN provider, does not stop malware you download.

Teaching notes for Unit III: explained in simple words

This part is written so that a teacher can read it aloud or explain it on the board, and a student can read it alone. Each idea starts with a story, then the proper definition, then what to write in the exam.

1. Why do we need encryption at all?

Imagine you send a postcard. Every postman who handles it can read it. Data on the internet is like that postcard: it passes through many routers and servers, and anyone on the way may read it. Encryption is like putting your message in a locked box before posting it. Only the person who has the key can open the box. In technical words, encryption converts the original readable message, called plaintext, into an unreadable form, called ciphertext, using a method (the algorithm) and a secret value (the key). Decryption does the opposite. The strength of a system depends on the key being secret, not on hiding the method. Everybody knows how AES works, yet it is safe because nobody knows your key.

Class activity

Ask a student to write a message using the Caesar cipher (shift every letter by 3, so A becomes D). Give the ciphertext to another student and ask them to break it. Then discuss why this is easy to break (only 25 possible shifts), and why modern keys with 2128 possibilities are not.

2. Encryption techniques: substitution and transposition

All old methods, and the building blocks of modern ones, are of two kinds. In a substitution cipher each letter is replaced by another letter or symbol. Example: with a shift of 3, HELLO becomes KHOOR. In a transposition cipher the letters stay the same but their positions are shuffled. Example: write WEB TECH in two rows and read by columns. A modern algorithm such as DES or AES repeats both operations many times, which is why it is called a product cipher. Ciphers are also divided into block ciphers, which cut the message into fixed-size pieces (for example 64 bits) and encrypt each piece, and stream ciphers, which encrypt bit by bit or byte by byte like a running tap.

3. Symmetric key encryption (secret key)

In symmetric encryption, the sender and receiver use the same key, both to lock and to unlock. It is like a door lock where both friends have a copy of the same key. It is very fast, so it is used to encrypt large files, videos and bank data. Its big weakness is the key distribution problem: how do you give the key to your friend without somebody stealing it on the way? If you send it by the same insecure network, the whole system fails. Also the number of keys grows quickly. If 10 people want to talk privately in pairs, they need 10 x 9 / 2 = 45 different keys; for 1000 people it is nearly five lakh keys. Examples of symmetric algorithms: DES, Triple DES, AES, Blowfish, IDEA, RC4.

4. Data Encryption Standard (DES)

DES was developed by IBM and adopted by the US government in 1977 as the official standard. It encrypts data in blocks of 64 bits using a key of 56 bits (the key is written as 64 bits, but 8 bits are only for error checking). The working can be explained in three stages. First, the 64-bit block goes through an initial permutation, which only shuffles the bit positions. Second, the block is split into a left half and a right half of 32 bits each, and then 16 rounds are performed. In every round, the right half is expanded to 48 bits, mixed with a 48-bit subkey made from the main key, passed through S-boxes (substitution tables that make the output hard to predict), shuffled again, and combined with the left half. The two halves are then swapped. This repeated structure is called a Feistel network, and its good point is that decryption uses the same steps with the subkeys in reverse order. Third, after round 16, a final permutation gives the ciphertext.

Why was DES retired? A 56-bit key has about 72 quadrillion possible values (256). In 1977 this looked huge, but modern computers and special machines can try all of them within hours. So DES is no longer safe, though it is the base for understanding later ciphers.

5. Triple DES (3DES)

When DES became weak, instead of designing something completely new at once, people simply ran DES three times. This is Triple DES. The usual method is Encrypt-Decrypt-Encrypt (EDE): encrypt with key K1, decrypt with key K2, and encrypt again with key K3. With three different keys the key length becomes 168 bits (effective strength about 112 bits); with K1 equal to K3 it uses two keys (112 bits). The decrypt step in the middle is there so that if all keys are the same, 3DES behaves like single DES, which keeps it compatible with old systems. The advantages are strong security and no need to change the old DES hardware. The disadvantage is that it is three times slower and still uses a small 64-bit block. Therefore it has been replaced by AES, which works on 128-bit blocks with 128, 192 or 256-bit keys and is both faster and stronger.

6. Asymmetric key encryption (public key)

Asymmetric encryption solves the key distribution problem with a clever idea: every person has two different keys that are mathematically linked. One is the public key, which can be given to anyone, even published in a newspaper. The other is the private key, which only the owner knows. Whatever is locked with one key can be unlocked only with the other. Think of a letter box on a gate. Anyone can drop a letter through the slot (public key), but only the owner who has the key to the box can take the letters out (private key). So if Asha wants to send a secret to Ravi, she encrypts with Ravi public key, and only Ravi, with his private key, can read it. No secret key ever travels on the network.

The price for this convenience is speed. Public key maths works on very large numbers and is about a hundred to a thousand times slower than symmetric encryption. So in real life both are combined. When you open a bank site, your browser uses public key cryptography only to agree on a small session key, and then uses fast symmetric encryption for the actual page. This is called hybrid encryption.

7. RSA with a small worked example

RSA is the most famous public key algorithm, invented in 1977 by Rivest, Shamir and Adleman. Its safety comes from a simple fact: multiplying two big prime numbers is easy, but finding the two primes from the product is extremely hard. To make a key pair: choose two primes p and q; compute n = p x q; compute phi = (p-1)(q-1); choose a public exponent e that has no common factor with phi; and find d such that (e x d) mod phi = 1. The public key is (e, n) and the private key is (d, n). Encryption is C = Me mod n and decryption is M = Cd mod n.

Example for the board: take p = 3 and q = 11. Then n = 33 and phi = 2 x 10 = 20. Choose e = 7. Since 7 x 3 = 21 and 21 mod 20 = 1, we get d = 3. Public key = (7, 33), private key = (3, 33). To send the message M = 4: C = 47 mod 33 = 16384 mod 33 = 16. The receiver computes 163 mod 33 = 4096 mod 33 = 4, which is the original message. In real life p and q have hundreds of digits, so n cannot be factored.

8. Digital signature: the story version

When you sign a cheque, your signature shows that you agreed to it, and nobody else can easily copy it. A digital signature does the same for electronic documents, but it is stronger because it is tied to the exact content. If one letter in the document changes, the signature becomes invalid. It gives three guarantees: authentication (the message really came from this person), integrity (nobody changed it), and non-repudiation (the sender cannot later say it was not him).

How it is made, step by step. Step 1: the sender runs the message through a hash function (for example SHA-256), which produces a short fixed-size fingerprint called the message digest. Any tiny change in the message gives a completely different digest. Step 2: the sender encrypts this digest with his own private key. The result is the digital signature. Step 3: the message, the signature and the sender certificate are sent together. How it is checked. Step 4: the receiver decrypts the signature with the sender public key and gets the original digest. Step 5: the receiver also hashes the received message himself. Step 6: if both digests are equal, the message is genuine and unchanged. If not, it was altered or not sent by that person.

Common confusion

For secrecy you encrypt with the receiver public key. For a signature you encrypt the hash with your own private key. A signature does not hide the message; it only proves who sent it and that it is unchanged. Students often reverse these two, so repeat it in class.

Why a certificate? Suppose a criminal posts a public key claiming to be your bank. How will you know? A Certifying Authority (CA), a trusted organisation, verifies the person and issues a digital certificate that binds the name to the public key and is itself signed by the CA. In India, CAs are licensed and controlled by the Controller of Certifying Authorities (CCA) under the IT Act. Certificates have an expiry date and can be revoked if the private key is lost.

9. Technical issues of digital signatures

Even a mathematically strong system can fail in practice. The biggest risk is the private key: if it is stolen, copied or forgotten, the owner is in trouble, so it must be kept in a protected place such as a USB token or smart card with a PIN. Weak hash functions are another risk; MD5 and SHA-1 are now broken because different messages can be made to produce the same digest (a collision), so SHA-2 or SHA-3 should be used. Keys must be long enough. Certificates can expire or be revoked, so the software must check the revocation list. There must be a trusted time stamp to show when something was signed. And the whole system depends on people trusting the CA, so the failure of a CA affects everyone. Finally, signatures must stay verifiable for many years, though algorithms get weaker with time.

10. Legal issues, electronic records and digital contracts

Before the IT Act 2000, an Indian court could ask: is an e-mail a document, is a typed name a signature, and is an online agreement a contract? The Act gave clear answers. Section 4 says that if the law requires something in writing, an electronic record satisfies it. Section 5 says that if the law requires a signature, a digital (later, electronic) signature is accepted. Section 3 explains how a record is authenticated with a digital signature, and Section 3A (added in 2008) widens this to any reliable electronic signature technique, such as Aadhaar e-Sign. Section 10A says that a contract made through electronic means is valid and cannot be denied only because it is electronic. Sections 6 to 8 allow government offices to accept forms, keep records and publish gazettes electronically. In a court case, an electronic record is accepted as evidence if it comes with the required certificate (Section 65B of the old Evidence Act, now Section 63 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam 2023).

For a digital contract to be valid, the usual elements of a contract must still be present: offer, acceptance, lawful consideration, capacity of the parties and free consent. Clicking an "I agree" button (click-wrap) is a common form of acceptance. The Act does not apply to some documents, such as wills, trusts, powers of attorney, sale of immovable property and negotiable instruments (other than a cheque), which still need paper and handwritten signatures. Problems that remain are proving the identity of the person who clicked, deciding which country court has jurisdiction, and protecting the private key from misuse.

11. Requirements of a good digital signature system

A signature system is considered reliable if it meets these conditions. The signature must be unique to the signer and created using something only the signer controls. It must be verifiable by anyone using the public key. It must be bound to the document, so that changing even one character is detected. It must be hard to forge and the private key must not be derivable from the public key. It must be based on standard, tested algorithms such as RSA or ECDSA with SHA-2. It needs a public key infrastructure (PKI): certifying authorities, certificate issue, renewal and revocation. It should support secure key storage, time-stamping and long-term validation, and it should be convenient enough that ordinary users can use it.

12. Virtual Private Network (VPN)

A company has an office in Kanpur and another in Pune. It could rent a private cable between them, but that is very expensive. A VPN offers a cheaper way: both offices use the ordinary internet, but all data between them is wrapped inside encrypted packets that travel through a secure tunnel. To outsiders the data looks like meaningless scrambled bits, while to the two offices it works as if they were on one private network. The word virtual means it behaves like a private line but is not a real physical line.

VPN uses two ideas together: tunnelling (putting one packet inside another) and encryption, plus authentication so that only authorised users can enter. Common protocols are IPsec (used between networks), SSL/TLS VPN (works from a browser), L2TP and WireGuard. The old PPTP is considered weak. There are two main types: remote-access VPN (an employee working from home connects to the office) and site-to-site VPN (two office networks are joined). Advantages: confidentiality over public networks, lower cost than leased lines, safe work from anywhere, and hiding your real IP address. Limitations: it is slower because of encryption, you must trust the VPN provider, and it does not protect you if your own computer already has a virus.

How to score in the exam

Start each answer with a one-line definition, then give a diagram or steps, then a table of comparison or an example, and end with advantages and limitations. For RSA and DES always show a small worked example or the round structure; these carry the marks.

Jurisprudence and the IT Act 2000

Jurisprudence is the philosophy and science of law. Cyber law is the body of law dealing with the internet, computers, electronic data and online conduct. Old law assumed paper, physical presence and borders, so new rules were needed for e-records, signatures, hacking and online contracts.

Information Technology Act, 2000 came into force on 17 October 2000, based on the UNCITRAL Model Law. It was substantially amended in 2008 (in force 2009).

Object and scope

  • Give legal recognition to electronic records and digital signatures, and to e-commerce transactions.
  • Facilitate e-filing with government and electronic payments.
  • Define and punish cyber offences and give powers of investigation.
  • Amend the IPC, Indian Evidence Act, Bankers Books Evidence Act and RBI Act.
  • Applies to the whole of India, and to offences committed outside India involving a computer in India (Sec 1(2), Sec 75).

UNCITRAL Model Law and ISP guidelines

UNCITRAL (UN Commission on International Trade Law) adopted the Model Law on Electronic Commerce in 1996 so countries could write consistent e-commerce laws. Core ideas: functional equivalence (an electronic record serves the same purpose as paper), non-discrimination against electronic form, and technology neutrality. UN General Assembly recommended it in 1996 and India followed it in drafting the IT Act. A 2001 Model Law covers electronic signatures.

ISP guidelines: ISPs (Internet Service Providers) need a licence from the Department of Telecommunications. Duties: keep user logs for a stated period, cooperate with lawful interception, block unlawful content on government order, protect user privacy, follow the due-diligence rules. Under Sec 79 an intermediary is not liable for third-party content if it only transmits, does not initiate, and acts promptly on takedown orders (IT Intermediary Guidelines 2021).

Intellectual property and cyberspace

Intellectual property (IP) is a creation of the mind protected by law. On the internet, copying costs nothing, so issues grow: piracy, framing and linking, caching, domain name disputes, software cloning, database copying, AI-generated works. Indian IP law follows the TRIPS agreement of the WTO.

Teaching notes for Unit IV: explained in simple words

1. What is cyber law and why did we need it?

Think of road traffic. When roads and vehicles came, we needed traffic rules, licences and fines. The internet is a new road, so it needed its own rules. Old laws were written for paper, shops and physical places. They could not answer questions like: is an e-mail a document, is a typed name a signature, who is punished when a hacker in one city damages a computer in another? Cyber law is the part of law that deals with computers, networks, electronic data and online behaviour. Jurisprudence simply means the study and philosophy of law, so "jurisprudence of cyber law" asks: what are the principles behind these rules? The main principles are that online and offline acts should be treated alike, that electronic records deserve the same trust as paper, that people must be protected from online harm, and that business should be able to run safely online.

2. The IT Act 2000: how it came and what it does

In 1996 the United Nations adopted a model law for e-commerce (UNCITRAL). Its idea was that every country should change its law so that electronic documents are not rejected only because they are electronic. India accepted this and passed the Information Technology Act, 2000, which came into force on 17 October 2000. In 2008 it was amended to add new crimes (identity theft, cyber terrorism, child abuse material), to widen digital signatures into electronic signatures, and to define the duties of intermediaries. The Act has about 90 sections.

Object (aim): to give legal recognition to electronic records and digital signatures; to make electronic filing and e-governance possible; to support e-commerce and banking; to define cyber offences and punishments; and to amend the Indian Penal Code, the Evidence Act, the Bankers Books Evidence Act and the RBI Act so that they match the digital world. Scope: it applies to the whole of India and also to any person who commits an offence outside India if the offence involves a computer or network located in India (Section 1(2) and Section 75). It does not apply to wills, trusts, powers of attorney, sale of immovable property and some negotiable instruments listed in the First Schedule.

Important parts to explain: Chapter II gives legal recognition to digital and electronic signatures. Chapter VI to VIII set up the Controller of Certifying Authorities and the rules for certificates. Section 43 makes a person who accesses a computer without permission, copies data, introduces a virus or denies access liable to pay compensation. Chapter XI lists the punishable offences: Section 65 (tampering with source code), 66 (computer-related offences), 66B to 66D (stolen devices, identity theft, cheating by impersonation), 66E (violating privacy by photographing private parts), 66F (cyber terrorism, up to life imprisonment), 67 to 67B (obscene, sexually explicit and child abuse material), 69 (government power to intercept or block), 70 (protected systems) and 72 (breach of confidentiality). Section 79 protects intermediaries, such as an ISP, from liability if they only pass on content and remove unlawful content when told. Disputes about compensation are heard by an adjudicating officer, and appeals go to the Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal (TDSAT), which took over from the Cyber Appellate Tribunal in 2017.

Update for students

Section 66A, which punished sending "offensive" messages, was struck down by the Supreme Court in Shreya Singhal v Union of India (2015) as against free speech. Also, from July 2024 the Indian Penal Code is replaced by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS) and the Evidence Act by the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA).

3. UNCITRAL Model Law

UNCITRAL means the United Nations Commission on International Trade Law. In 1996 it published the Model Law on Electronic Commerce. A model law is not binding, it is a sample that countries copy into their own law so that rules in different countries become similar, which helps international trade. Its three main principles are easy to remember. Functional equivalence: an electronic record does the same job as paper (writing, signature, original, evidence), so the law should accept it. Non-discrimination: a record must not be refused only because it is in electronic form. Technology neutrality: the law should not favour one technology, so it stays useful when technology changes. It deals with formation of contracts by data messages, time and place of sending and receiving, acknowledgement of receipt and carriage of goods documents. A second model law on Electronic Signatures followed in 2001. India used the 1996 law as the base of the IT Act 2000.

4. ISP guidelines in India

An Internet Service Provider (ISP) is the company that connects you to the internet. In India an ISP needs a licence from the Department of Telecommunications. The licence conditions require the ISP to keep records (logs) of who used the network for a period of time, help the government with lawful interception and monitoring, block websites when ordered under Section 69A, protect the privacy of customers, not allow illegal content knowingly, and maintain quality of service. Under Section 79 of the IT Act and the IT (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021, an intermediary is free from liability for what users post only if it follows due diligence: publish rules and privacy policy, appoint a grievance officer, remove unlawful content within the time given after a court or government order, and help investigating agencies. Big social media companies have extra duties such as a compliance officer and tracing the first originator of a message in serious cases.

5. Intellectual property and cyberspace

Intellectual property (IP) means creations of the human mind, like inventions, books, music, software, brand names and designs. The law gives the creator an exclusive right so others cannot copy or use it without permission. Think of IP as a plot of land in the world of ideas, and the IP law as the fence around it. Cyberspace makes IP protection difficult because copying a file takes a second and costs nothing, and a copy is as good as the original. The main problems are software and music piracy, copying content from websites, unauthorised linking and framing of other pages, cybersquatting (registering a famous name as a domain to sell it back), copying a website design, and misuse of trademarks in meta tags or advertisements. India follows the TRIPS Agreement of the World Trade Organisation, which sets minimum IP standards for all member countries.

6. Patent law

A patent is an exclusive right given by the government for an invention, in exchange for the inventor disclosing how it works. The invention must be new (novel), involve an inventive step (not obvious to an expert) and be capable of industrial application. The law is the Patents Act, 1970 (major amendment in 2005 to follow TRIPS). Protection lasts 20 years from the date of filing, after which anyone can use the invention. The patent is filed at the Patent Office (Mumbai, Delhi, Chennai, Kolkata). The owner can stop others from making, using, selling or importing the invention, and can sell or license it. Many things are not patentable, such as discoveries, mathematical methods, business methods, and computer programs per se (Section 3(k)); however, software that gives a technical effect, as part of a machine or process, may be patented. Example: a new method of data compression built into a hardware device could qualify, while a plain algorithm cannot.

7. Copyright law

Copyright protects the expression of an idea, not the idea. It covers literary works (including computer programs and databases), music, drama, art, films and sound recordings. In India the law is the Copyright Act, 1957 (amended in 1994 to cover software, and in 2012). Copyright arises automatically when the work is created; registration is optional but helps in court. The term is generally the author life plus 60 years. The owner has the right to copy, distribute, adapt, translate and perform the work. Others may use it under fair dealing for study, research, criticism, news reporting and teaching. Copying without permission is infringement, with remedies of injunction, damages, and punishment of 6 months to 3 years jail and a fine under Section 63. Buying software gives you a licence to use it, not the right to copy and sell it. Open source licences (GPL, MIT) give users extra freedoms but are still licences under copyright.

8. Trademark law

A trademark is a word, name, logo, symbol, colour combination or shape that tells customers who made a product or service, for example the Tata name or the Amul girl. The law is the Trade Marks Act, 1999. A mark must be distinctive and is registered with the Trade Marks Registry. Registration is valid for 10 years and can be renewed for 10 years again and again, so a trademark can last forever. The owner can stop others using a confusingly similar mark and sue for infringement; even an unregistered mark can be protected by the common-law action of passing off. On the internet the main conflict is the domain name. Since one domain can be held by only one person, a person may register a well-known brand as a domain (cybersquatting). Remedies are a court case, the ICANN UDRP process through WIPO, or the .IN Domain Name Dispute Resolution Policy.

9. Semiconductor layout and design law

Every computer chip is made with a tiny pattern of transistors and wires, called its layout-design or topography. Designing it takes huge money and time, yet copying it is easy. India therefore passed the Semiconductor Integrated Circuits Layout-Design Act, 2000 to protect original layouts. The layout must be original, not commonplace, and registered with the Registrar of the layout-design registry. Registration gives exclusive rights for 10 years. Copying the design, or importing and selling chips made with a copied layout, is an offence with imprisonment and fine. The Act is separate from patents and copyright because the layout is a special kind of work.

Cyber crime and jurisdiction

Cyber crime is any illegal act where a computer or network is the target, the tool or the place of the crime. Examples: hacking, phishing, identity theft, online fraud, cyber stalking, cyber terrorism, child abuse material, ransomware.

Cyber jurisdiction: which court may try a crime when the offender, victim and server sit in different places? Tests: place of the offender, place of the victim or computer affected, effects doctrine. India: Sec 1(2) and Sec 75 of the IT Act extend to offences outside India affecting a computer in India; Sec 46 gives adjudication powers; CrPC allows trial where the effect occurred. Extradition and mutual legal assistance treaties are needed to reach the offender, and the Budapest Convention (2001) is the main international treaty (India has not signed it).

Treatment in different countries: USA: Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, 1986. UK: Computer Misuse Act, 1990. EU: GDPR for data and NIS directives. India: IT Act 2000 and BNS 2023. Singapore: Computer Misuse Act. Differences in laws make cross-border prosecution hard.

Cyber crime and evidence

Evidence is digital: logs, e-mails, files, CCTV, call records. It is fragile, easy to alter, so chain of custody, hash values (to prove the copy equals the original), write-blockers and forensic imaging are used. The IT Act amended the Evidence Act so electronic records are documents; a certificate (Sec 65B, now Sec 63 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam 2023) is needed to admit computer output. Digital signature certificates and the Cyber Forensic labs support investigation. Sec 78 allows Inspector-rank police to investigate IT Act offences; Sec 80 allows search without a warrant in public places.

Ethical issues: privacy, plagiarism, pornography, tampering

  • Data and software privacy: collecting, using, selling personal data without consent; spyware; tracking cookies. Law: Sec 43A and 72 IT Act, Puttaswamy judgement (2017, privacy a fundamental right), Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 (consent, purpose limitation, rights of data principals, penalties).
  • Plagiarism: presenting another work as your own. Violates copyright and academic ethics; detected by tools like Turnitin.
  • Pornography: Sec 67 (obscene), 67A (sexually explicit), 67B (child abuse material) of the IT Act; POCSO Act 2012. Possession and transmission of child material are serious crimes.
  • Tampering with computer documents: Sec 65 punishes concealing, destroying or altering source code kept by law: up to 3 years, fine up to Rs 2 lakh, or both.
  • Domain Name System issues: cybersquatting, typosquatting, domain hijacking; remedies by trademark action and WIPO or .IN dispute policy.
  • Software piracy: unlicensed copying (end-user piracy, counterfeiting, internet piracy, softlifting). Copyright Act Sec 63 and 63B: jail up to 3 years and fine.

Ethical hacking

Ethical hacking (penetration testing) is authorised attempts to break into a system to find weaknesses before criminals do. Hackers: white hat (ethical, with permission), black hat (criminal), grey hat (no permission but no harm intended). Phases: reconnaissance, scanning, gaining access, maintaining access, clearing tracks, then a report. Ethical issues: must have written permission and a defined scope, keep findings confidential, avoid damage, disclose responsibly. Without authorisation it is still an offence (Sec 43 and 66). Certifications: CEH, OSCP.

Teaching notes for Unit V: explained in simple words

1. Cyber crime: meaning and kinds

A normal crime like theft happens in a place. A cyber crime is a crime where a computer or network is the target (hacking a server), the tool (sending a fraud e-mail) or the place where evidence lives. The IT Act does not define the term in one line, it lists the offences. Cyber crimes are grouped by victim. Against persons: cyber stalking, harassment, defamation, morphing photos, sextortion. Against property: data theft, online banking fraud, phishing, ransomware, software piracy. Against government and society: cyber terrorism, attacks on power or banking systems, spreading hate, obscene content. Criminals love cyberspace because it is cheap, fast, global and the attacker can hide behind false identities.

2. Cyber jurisdiction

Jurisdiction means which court has the power to hear a case. Suppose a hacker in Country A uses a server in Country B to steal money from a bank in India. Who will try him? This is the central problem of cyber crime, because the internet has no borders but law does. Courts use tests such as: where the offender was, where the victim or the damaged computer was, where the server was, or where the effect was felt (the effects doctrine). In India, Section 1(2) and Section 75 of the IT Act apply to an offence outside India if it involves a computer in India; the Criminal Procedure Code allows a case to be heard where the effect occurred. To catch an offender abroad, India needs extradition treaties and mutual legal assistance from the other country. The main international agreement is the Budapest Convention on Cybercrime (2001), which India has not signed.

3. Treatment of cyber crime in different countries

Every country has its own law, which causes gaps that criminals use. The USA has the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (1986) and many state laws. The UK has the Computer Misuse Act (1990), which punishes unauthorised access and modification. The European Union follows the Budapest Convention and has the strong data law GDPR (2018). Singapore has its Computer Misuse and Cybersecurity Act. India uses the IT Act 2000 together with the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita for offences such as cheating and forgery. Differences exist in what counts as a crime, the punishment, and how evidence is collected, so global cooperation is needed.

4. Cyber crime and evidence

In a cyber case, the proof is digital evidence: log files, e-mails, chat messages, files on a hard disk, CCTV, call records and mobile data. It is fragile because it can be changed or deleted in seconds, and a copy looks exactly like the original. So investigators follow strict steps. First identify and secure the device without switching anything on. Then make a forensic image (an exact copy) using a write-blocker so the original is not altered. Calculate a hash value (a fingerprint) of the original and the copy; if they match, the copy is exact. Keep a written chain of custody showing who handled the evidence, when and why. Then analyse the copy and prepare a report. In court, the IT Act changed the Evidence Act so that electronic records are accepted as documents, but a certificate from the person in charge of the device (Section 65B, now Section 63 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam 2023) must say how the output was produced. Under Section 78 of the IT Act an officer of the rank of Inspector can investigate, and Section 80 allows entry and search of a public place without a warrant.

5. Ethical issues in data and software privacy

Ethics means what is right, even when no law forces us. Data privacy is a person right to control how personal information (name, phone, Aadhaar, health, location, browsing) is collected and used. Companies collect data through forms, cookies and apps, and ethical questions arise: did the user know and agree, is only the needed data collected, is it kept safe, is it sold to others? Software privacy problems include spyware, hidden trackers and apps asking for more permissions than needed. In India the Supreme Court in Puttaswamy (2017) held that privacy is a fundamental right. Section 43A (compensation for failing to protect sensitive data) and Section 72 (breach of confidentiality) of the IT Act protect data, and the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 now requires consent, use only for the stated purpose, the right to correct and erase data, breach notification and heavy penalties on companies.

6. Plagiarism, pornography and tampering

Plagiarism is using another person words, ideas or code and presenting them as your own, without credit. The internet makes copy-paste easy, so students and professionals must learn to quote, cite and paraphrase. It is an ethical wrong, and when the work is protected it is also copyright infringement. Pornography is dealt with under Section 67 (publishing or sending obscene material: up to 3 years for the first offence), 67A (sexually explicit acts: up to 5 years) and 67B (child abuse material: up to 5 years, 7 on repeat), along with the POCSO Act, 2012, which punishes using children in such material. Even keeping or forwarding child material is a crime. Tampering with computer source documents (Section 65) means knowingly hiding, destroying or changing source code that the law requires to be kept; it is punished with up to 3 years or a fine up to Rs 2 lakh, or both.

7. Domain Name System issues and software piracy

The Domain Name System (DNS) turns names like google.com into IP addresses. Because names are valuable, disputes arise: cybersquatting (registering a brand name to sell it at a high price), typosquatting (registering a misspelt name such as gogle.com to trap visitors), domain hijacking (taking over someone domain), and DNS spoofing (giving false answers to send users to a fake site). Remedies are a trademark case or a complaint under the UDRP or the .IN policy. Software piracy is the unauthorised copying, use or sale of software. Types: softlifting (installing one licensed copy on many computers), counterfeiting (selling fake copies), internet piracy (downloading cracked software or torrents), hard-disk loading and client-server overuse (more users than licences). It is an offence under Sections 63 and 63B of the Copyright Act (6 months to 3 years jail and a fine of Rs 50,000 to Rs 2 lakh). Pirated software also carries viruses, has no updates and exposes users to legal risk.

8. Issues in ethical hacking

A hacker is a person with deep computer skill who finds weaknesses. White hat hackers do it with permission to improve security, black hat hackers do it to steal or damage, and grey hat hackers enter without permission but do not intend harm and may report the flaw. Ethical hacking (penetration testing) is the authorised testing of a system. It follows phases: reconnaissance (collect information), scanning (find open ports and weak points), gaining access, maintaining access, covering tracks (to test whether the owner could detect it), and finally a written report with fixes. The issues are: written permission and a fixed scope are essential, because without them it is a crime under Sections 43 and 66; the tester must keep client data confidential and not damage the system; vulnerabilities must be reported responsibly; and there is a debate whether grey hat acts, bug bounty programs and hiring former criminals are acceptable.

9. Internet security threats

Hacking is gaining unauthorised access to a system, usually by using a flaw or a stolen password. Cracking is breaking a protection, such as guessing passwords or removing the licence check of software, with criminal intent. Sneaking means secretly watching or entering: shoulder surfing, spyware and packet sniffing. A virus is a program that attaches itself to a file and spreads when that file is run, just as a biological virus needs a host cell. A worm spreads by itself across networks without needing a host file or any user action, so it can infect thousands of computers quickly (the ILOVEYOU and WannaCry cases). A Trojan horse pretends to be useful software, like a free game, but secretly opens a back door or steals data, named after the wooden horse of Troy; it does not copy itself. Malicious code or malware is the general name for all harmful software, including ransomware (locks files for money), spyware, adware, rootkits and botnets. A logic bomb is code hidden in a program that stays quiet until a condition is met, for example a date or the deletion of an employee record from a payroll, and then it deletes data or crashes the system. Protection: antivirus and updates, patches, firewall, backups, strong passwords with two-factor login, no pirated software, no unknown attachments, and user training.

10. Biometric security, fingerprint and the challenges

Passwords can be forgotten or stolen, so some systems identify a person by who they are. Biometrics uses body traits (fingerprint, face, iris, retina, palm, DNA) or behaviour (voice, signature, typing style). A good trait is universal, unique, permanent and easy to measure. Aadhaar, phone unlock and office attendance use it. Fingerprint is the most common. A sensor takes the image of ridges and valleys; software finds minutiae points, such as where a ridge ends or splits, and turns them into a template. At enrolment the template is stored; at verification a fresh scan is compared with it and the system decides match or no match. The system can make two errors: false accept (wrong person allowed in) and false reject (right person refused); lowering one raises the other.

Challenges: a leaked fingerprint cannot be changed, unlike a password. Fake fingers made of gelatin or photos can fool simple sensors, so liveness detection is needed. Dirty, wet, worn or injured fingers cause false rejects; scanners are costly; stored templates are a target for hackers, so they must be encrypted; privacy and consent issues arise when data is collected without clear permission; and face recognition can be biased and inaccurate across groups. Best practice is to combine biometrics with a PIN (multi-factor), store templates encrypted and, if possible, on the device itself.
